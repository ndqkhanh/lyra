**Authors:** Yijia Shao\*, Humishka Zope\*, Yucheng Jiang, Jiaxin Pei, David Nguyen, Erik Brynjolfsson, Diyi Yang (Stanford University)
**Venue:** arXiv:2506.06576v3 [cs.CY], Feb 2026
**Data & Code:** futureofwork.saltlab.stanford.edu

## 1. Mechanism (step-by-step)

### 1.1 Auditing Framework Design

The paper introduces a **principled, survey-based auditing framework** that operates at the **task level** (not occupation level) to assess AI agent automation/augmentation potential. The framework has four design pillars:

**Pillar 1 — Audit Granularity (Task-Level):**
- Sources occupational tasks from the U.S. Department of Labor's O\*NET database (version 29.2).
- Starting from 18,796 task statements across 923 occupations, the authors filter to computer-compatible tasks performed at least monthly:
  - Step A: GPT-4o judges whether the occupation "mainly involves using computers" (Yes/No).
  - Step B: GPT-4o judges whether the specific task "can be finished on a computer" (Yes/No).
  - Step C: Tasks must have Core/Supplementary annotation and frequency > monthly.
- Result: **2,131 tasks across 287 occupations** remain after filtering.
- Defines AI agents (excluding physical robots) as: "A system or program capable of autonomously performing tasks on behalf of a user or another system by designing its workflow and utilizing available software tools."

**Pillar 2 — Human Agency Scale (HAS):**
- A novel 5-level scale (H1-H5) quantifying the spectrum from automation to augmentation:
  - **H1:** AI agent handles task entirely on its own (no human involvement).
  - **H2:** AI agent needs minimal human input for optimal performance.
  - **H3:** AI agent and human form equal partnership, outperforming either alone.
  - **H4:** AI agent requires human input to successfully complete the task.
  - **H5:** AI agent cannot function without continuous human involvement.
- H1-H2 favor automation approaches; H3-H5 favor augmentation strategies.
- Complements SAE L0-L5 driving automation levels but from a human-centered lens.

**Pillar 3 — Audio-Enhanced Worker-Centric Survey:**
- Workers recruited via Prolific (1,060), Upwork (184), LinkedIn/word-of-mouth (434).
- Survey begins with an **audio-enhanced mini-interview**: 5 open-ended questions exploring their work process, tools, and AI perspectives. Spoken responses are transcribed; workers can edit transcripts in real time.
- Task rating section collects two key metrics per task `t` from worker `w`:
  - **Automation Desire `A_w(t)`**: 5-point Likert scale ("If an AI can do this task for you completely, how much do you want it to do it for you?": 1=Not at all to 5=Entirely).
  - **Desired HAS Level `H_w(t)`**: 5-point Likert mapping to H1-H5 ("If AI were to assist in this task, how much collaboration would be needed?").
- Workers must confirm task familiarity before rating (quality control).
- Before rating, workers explicitly consider: job loss concern, enjoyment, physical actions, domain expertise, uncertainty, and interpersonal communication.

**Pillar 4 — Dual-Perspective Assessment:**
- **52 AI experts** (PhD students, postdocs, professors, industry practitioners from Stanford, MIT, Google, xAI, etc.) independently rate each task for:
  - **Current Automation Capability `A_e(t)`**: The same 5-point Likert scale.
  - **Feasible HAS Level `H_e(t)`**: The same H1-H5 rubric.
- Each task assessed by at least 2 experts; additional reviews until standard deviation <= 1.
- Inter-annotator agreement (Krippendorff's alpha): 0.539 for `A_e(t)`, 0.511 for `H_e(t)`.

### 1.2 WORKBank Database Construction

After filtering for >= 10 participants per occupation (Jan-May 2025):
- **1,500 domain workers** across **104 occupations** retained.
- **844 tasks** with worker and expert ratings.
- **7,016 total task ratings** collected.

### 1.3 Four-Zone Desire-Capability Landscape

Joint visualization of `A_w(t)` (x-axis: worker automation desire) vs `A_e(t)` (y-axis: expert-assessed capability) divides tasks into four quadrants:

1. **Automation "Green Light" Zone** — High desire + High capability. Prime candidates for deployment.
2. **Automation "Red Light" Zone** — High capability + Low desire. Caution warranted; worker resistance likely.
3. **R&D Opportunity Zone** — High desire + Low capability. Promising research directions.
4. **Low Priority Zone** — Low desire + Low capability. Lower urgency.

Spearman correlation between `A_w(t)` and `A_e(t)`: rho = 0.17 (weak), p < 1e-6.

### 1.4 Investment/Research Alignment Analysis

- **YC Company mapping**: 5,156 companies retrieved (April 2025). GPT-4.1-mini classified 1,723 as AI-related. Each mapped to WORKBank tasks via binary classification prompt.
- **arXiv paper mapping**: 17,064 papers with "language model" + ("agent" or "system") in title/abstract. GPT-4.1-mini filtered to 1,222 genuine AI agent papers via a 4-criterion checklist. Each mapped to tasks.

### 1.5 Skill Shift Analysis

- Maps each O\*NET task to Generalized Work Activities (GWAs/skills) via the DWA-IWA-GWA hierarchy.
- Computes **average wage** (BLS May 2024 data) and **average expert-assessed human agency `H_e(t)`** per skill.
- Ranks skills by both metrics and compares rankings to identify shifts.

### 1.6 Statistical Robustness

- **Mixed-effects model**: `y_ij = beta_0 + sum(beta_k * X_k,ij) + u_j + epsilon_ij`
  - `u_j ~ N(0, sigma_u^2)`: task-specific random intercept
  - `epsilon_ij ~ N(0, sigma_epsilon^2)`: residual error
  - ICC = 0.050 (full dataset), ICC = 0.405 (high-signal subset of 238 tasks)
- **LLooM topic modeling** (Claude 3 Opus distillation + clustering) on audio transcripts.

## 3. Trade-offs

| Win | Cost / Trade-off |
|-----|------------------|
| Task-level granularity captures nuanced reality within occupations | O\*NET tasks are static snapshots; cannot capture new tasks that emerge with AI adoption |
| Worker-centric design surfaces latent preferences invisible in usage logs (Claude.ai data covers only 1.26% of usage for top-desire occupations) | Workers may have limited awareness of AI capabilities, potentially mis-calibrating their ratings |
| Audio-enhanced mini-interview yields rich qualitative narratives alongside quantitative ratings | Substantially more expensive and slower to collect at scale vs. text-only surveys |
| Dual worker + expert perspectives reveal critical misalignments (47.5% of tasks have workers preferring higher agency) | Expert inter-annotator agreement is moderate (alpha ~0.51-0.54), suggesting substantial subjectivity in capability assessments |
| HAS provides a shared language spanning automation-to-augmentation | Higher HAS is not inherently "better" — the scale requires careful interpretation per context |
| Four-zone landscape offers actionable investment/research targeting | Zone boundaries depend on thresholds that can shift with technological progress; the static snapshot ages quickly |
| Covers 104 occupations with demographic representativeness | Does not cover 183 of the 287 computer-using occupations identified; findings may not generalize to uncovered occupations |
| Mixed-effects model isolates task-level signal (ICC=0.405 in high-signal subset) | Full-dataset ICC of 0.050 means 95% of variance is individual-level; task signal is modest overall |
| Identifies R&D opportunities beyond current investment concentration (41% of YC companies in Low Priority + Red Light) | YC companies and arXiv papers are imperfect proxies for actual investment and research effort |

## 4. Design Rationale

The authors argue that prior work on AI's labor impact suffers from three gaps: (1) narrow domain coverage (mostly software engineering/customer support), (2) capital-centric framing that ignores worker values, and (3) reliance on retrospective usage data rather than forward-looking assessment. Their framework addresses all three: O\*NET provides full workforce coverage; the worker-centric survey (with audio interface for natural reflection) elevates worker voice; and expert assessments provide forward-looking capability estimates. The task-level (vs. occupation-level) granularity is crucial because tasks within the same occupation vary dramatically in their suitability for automation. The HAS scale was designed explicitly to move beyond the "automate-or-not" dichotomy that dominates prior literature, recognizing that AI agents hold significant potential for augmentation through human-agent collaboration (building on Collaborative Gym, Shao et al. 2024b).

## 5. Limitations and Failure Modes

1. **Static task definitions**: O\*NET captures existing occupational tasks and cannot account for novel tasks that emerge as AI agents integrate into workflows. The framework provides a baseline, not a dynamic forecast.

2. **Worker awareness gap**: Despite guided reflection prompts, domain workers may lack full understanding of AI agent capabilities and limitations, potentially skewing both automation desire and HAS ratings. The mixed-effects model's low ICC (0.050) suggests most rating variance is individual-level rather than task-level.

3. **Limited occupation coverage**: Only 104 of 287 computer-using occupations are represented (filtered for >= 10 participants). The database skews toward white-collar, computer-intensive professions and does not cover the full workforce spectrum.

4. **Moderate expert agreement**: Krippendorff's alpha values of 0.511-0.539 indicate substantial subjectivity in expert assessments of AI capability for specific tasks. This is an inherent challenge in forward-looking technology assessment.

5. **Temporal specificity**: Data collected Jan-May 2025 reflects AI capabilities of that moment. As foundational models and agent architectures evolve rapidly (the paper was revised Feb 2026), the capability landscape may already have shifted, particularly for tasks in the R&D Opportunity Zone.

6. **Crowdsourcing biases**: Prolific/Upwork participants may not perfectly represent the broader workforce, and workers may withhold honest feedback due to job security concerns despite privacy assurances.

7. **Imperfect investment/research proxies**: YC companies and arXiv papers are noisy proxies for actual investment and research activity. The LLM-based mapping pipeline (GPT-4.1-mini) introduces its own classification errors.

## 6. Transfer to Lyra

**The One Idea:** The **Human Agency Scale (HAS)** as a design-time rubric for Lyra's task routing and agent architecture decisions. Rather than treating all tasks as candidates for full automation, Lyra should classify each incoming task on an H1-H5 spectrum and adapt its execution mode accordingly: H1-H2 tasks can be handled autonomously by the agent, H3 tasks should surface intermediate results for human review (equal partnership), and H4-H5 tasks should operate in augmentation mode where Lyra acts as an intelligent assistant rather than an autonomous executor.

**§4 Workstream Route:** §4.1 (Agent Architecture & Control Loop) — specifically modifying the core execution loop to incorporate HAS-aware routing and human-in-the-loop gating at task boundaries.

**Impact:** 4/5 — This directly addresses Lyra's core design question of "when to automate vs. augment" and provides an empirically validated framework from a large-scale study (1,500 workers, 844 tasks).

**Effort:** 3/5 — Requires modifying the agent execution loop, adding a task-classification pre-pass, and implementing human-in-the-loop interfaces for H3-H5 tasks. Non-trivial but bounded.

**Tier:** **breakthrough** — The HAS framework provides a principled, empirically grounded taxonomy for hybrid autonomous-augmented agent design that is directly applicable to Lyra's architecture.
